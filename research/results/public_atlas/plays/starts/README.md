# Where the points come from after each way of getting the ball

From 1997 to 2026, teams scored 113.8 points per 100 possessions after a steal and 95.8 after an opponent's basket, a gap of 18.0. Every possession counts, including those that ended without a shot.

## The gap between a steal and an opponent's basket

| Part | Gap in points per 100 possessions | 95% range |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Layups and dunks | +25.7 | +25.1 to +26.2 |
| Other two point shots | -6.7 | -7.1 to -6.2 |
| Three point shots | -4.7 | -5.2 to -4.2 |
| Free throws | +3.4 | +3.1 to +3.8 |
| Second chances | +0.3 | -0.1 to +0.7 |

After a steal teams took 34.2 first chance layups and dunks per 100 possessions and made 68.5% of them. After an opponent's basket they took 18.5 and made 57.3%. Of the gap from layups and dunks, taking more of them accounts for +19.8 (+19.3 to +20.2) points per 100 possessions and making more of them for +5.9 (+5.6 to +6.2).

For 2022 to 2026 alone the total gap is 19.4.

## How it is counted

Possessions come from the play by play reconstruction, which keeps only possessions whose start, events and end could be confirmed, in regular season and playoff games. A shot is a layup or dunk when the league event file describes it as a layup, dunk or tip. First chance means before any offensive rebound in the possession. Second chances are all points after the first offensive rebound, from any shot or free throw. Free throws are the remaining first chance points. Ranges come from drawing whole games again 2,000 times. The split of the layup gap into volume and finishing averages the two orders of the comparison. These are descriptions of what happened and do not show that forcing more steals would add the same points.
