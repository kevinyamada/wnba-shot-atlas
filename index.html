    What is counted in the map?

    The map uses recorded regular season shots, with coordinates in whole feet. It includes eight games where some player attempt counts disagree with the supplied box scores; prediction comparisons exclude those games. Team totals agree. Tied scoring leaders are shown together.

    The Bayesian map uses a league reference player and average training context. It shows a fitted probability at each square’s centre, rather than a team’s observed make rate or a ranking of player skill. Striped squares in the observed rate view have fewer than 20 attempts.

    Counts, uncertainty and band boundaries

    The two foot comparison uses 508 attempts inside and 4,380 outside, adjusted for the area of each band. The density ratio is 7.97; its 95% game bootstrap interval is 7.25 to 8.82, using 2,000 resamples.

    The ratio uses −2 ≤ distance < 0 inside and 0 < distance ≤ 2 outside. The chart’s one foot bars include their lower endpoint and exclude their upper endpoint. The 140 shots exactly on the line enter the first outside bar; the 374 exactly two feet outside enter the third bar. For that reason, the first two outside bars do not sum to the ratio’s outside count. Whole foot coordinates also limit how precisely these bands can be interpreted.

    Predicting a make

    Distance alone is hard to beat

    Take two shots from 15 feet away. A model that uses only distance gives them the same chance of going in. Other models can take into account where on the court a shot came from, who took it, or the game situation.

    On 5,626 shots from late in the season, averaging five models’ predictions reduced prediction error by about 0.7% compared with using distance alone. The playoff results were too close to call.

    Distance also gave the best predictions in two of the three tests on parts of the court the models hadn’t seen. A model that uses nearby shots had a small lead in the third. I’d start with distance and look for a clearer gain in another season before relying on a more complicated model.

    Spatial model details and scores

    The chart compares distance alone, a Matérn SPDE model, and universal kriging. The SPDE model represents a smooth spatial effect on a triangular mesh; a sparse Gaussian Markov prior makes fitting practical. Universal kriging combines a distance trend with a spatial covariance model.

    After the shot

    A miss doesn’t always end the chance

    Some misses lead to another scoring opportunity. The event records let us follow ordinary play through offensive rebounds and free throws until the team’s turn ends.

    How the remaining points model works

    The model estimates the ordinary points still to come at the start of a team’s turn, after a miss, or after an offensive rebound. These turns are reconstructed from events; the archive does not supply official possession IDs.

    Coverage and calibration

    Strict checks retain 662 of 1,257 periods, or 52.7%, and 25,306 entry states. Special free throws, overturned reviews, clock or score conflicts, and uncertain team control can exclude a period. This screening uses the completed event record, so the retained sample may differ from excluded play.

    All 624 game/team final scores reconcile. Every retained score margin agrees with its source snapshot. Sixteen intervals end at a jump ball change without an intermediate event; they have zero recorded reward and do not establish an additional official possession. Predictions after an event use only information available up to that event.

    What would move this forward?

    A new WNBA season would allow a fresh prediction test. To value passes and movement, the next dataset would also need to record where those actions start and end. Those locations are missing from this archive.

    Every basketball observation here comes from the supplied Data (1).zip. The study is exploratory; it does not measure defensive pressure or assign causal player credit.
